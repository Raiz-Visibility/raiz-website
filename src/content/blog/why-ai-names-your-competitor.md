Ask ChatGPT for the best plumber in your town and you'll get two or three names. If yours isn't one of them, it's tempting to assume the model just prefers bigger companies. It doesn't. It prefers businesses it can confirm.

That confirmation happens in three steps. Each one is a place where good businesses quietly drop out.

## 1. It has to know who you are

First the model works out which business you are — one specific company, not one of four in the county with a similar name. If your name, address and phone number differ between your site, your Google profile and the directories, it can't be sure. When it isn't sure, it moves on.

## 2. It has to find you somewhere else

Your own website is a claim. The model wants to see that claim repeated in places you don't control: local news, trade associations, supplier pages, review sites. A business that only exists on its own domain looks thin, however good the site is.

> Not because you're not good — because the system couldn't confirm it.

## 3. It has to believe you

Finally, it checks that what you say holds up. If your site says you serve six towns but every review mentions one, or you list a service no one has ever reviewed you for, the story doesn't hang together.

## Where to start

You don't need to fix everything at once. Start with the pieces the model checks first:

- Make your name, address and phone identical everywhere they appear.
- List every service and town on your Google profile — and on its own page on your site.
- Get mentioned in two or three places locally that aren't directories.
- Ask for reviews that name the job and the town.

Do those four and you've closed most of the gap between being good and being named.
